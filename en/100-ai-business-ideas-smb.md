## Section A. 10 monetization models: how to get paid

Before you pick an idea, pick a **model**. The model decides your cash flow, how far you can grow, and what your workdays will look like.

🎨 **Picture this:** a sit-down restaurant, a coffee shop, a food truck and a catering company. They all sell food, but their economics, their rhythm and their ceiling are completely different. An AI business works the same way: how you get paid matters more than the "product" itself.

---

### Model 1: One-off project (pay, receive, done)

**What it is:** the client pays **a one-time fee** for a finished result. You do it, hand it over, and move on to the next one.

🎨 **Picture this:** a builder. Build the house, get paid, move to the next job site.

**When it works:**

- A clear scope of work (a landing page, an audit, a course outline)
- An easy-to-understand deliverable
- A fast turnaround (1-4 weeks)
- B2B (services for companies) and freelance markets

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- Unpredictable cash flow (feast or famine)
- The business itself doesn't gain value over time
- You have to find every new client from scratch

**Who does this already:**

- Top freelancers on Upwork and Fiverr
- DesignJoy
- 99designs (logo contests)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- AI-built landing page: $500-3K
- AI audit (SEO, meaning search visibility; code; brand): $1-5K
- AI-assisted course outline and production: $5-25K
- Setting up AI automation: $2-15K
- AI rollout at a large company: $20-200K

**Cash flow:** ❌ uneven: one month is great, the next one is slow. To ride out the dips, you need 3-6 months of cash in reserve.

**Difficulty:** 🟢 easy to start | 🔴 hard to scale

---

### Model 2: Monthly subscription (SaaS)

**What it is:** the client subscribes and pays every month for as long as the product is useful to them.

🎨 **Picture this:** Netflix. You pay for the subscription and you watch. Stop watching, cancel. But many people keep paying for months out of habit (that's part of what the model rests on).

**When it works:**

- The client's problem is ongoing (not one-time)
- The client uses the product every day or every week
- Leaving is costly for the client (their data, their routine; this is called lock-in)
- LTV (how much a client pays you over their whole lifetime) > 3 × CAC (what it costs to win one client)

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- Problems that get solved once (writing a resume, writing a wedding toast)
- High churn (more than 10% of clients leaving per month)
- A price that's too low (the $9/month trap)

**Who does this already:**

- Notion
- Linear
- Granola (AI meeting notes)
- Cursor (AI for programming)
- Tally (forms)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Small independent tool: $9-29/month
- Tool for small businesses: $49-149/month
- Professional tool: $99-499/month
- For large companies: $500-5K/month

**Cash flow:** 🟢 predictable: it becomes stable at roughly 100 paying clients.

**Difficulty:** 🔴 hard (a long ramp-up) | 🟢 easier to scale (once the product finds its market, growth comes faster)

---

### Model 3: Pay as you go (API resale, voice call minutes)

**What it is:** the client pays **for usage**: per minute, per request, per message, per token (the unit of text AI uses to measure volume).

🎨 **Picture this:** a cab ride. You pay for the trip. No rides, no bill. But heavy users pay a lot.

**When it works:**

- Clients use it in very different amounts (heavy users 10 times more than occasional ones)
- A clear billing unit (a call, a minute, a message, a document)
- A markup above 60% (what you pay for the API, meaning access to someone else's AI service, is well below your price)
- Large companies with budgets

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- Unpredictable usage spikes lead to a furious client looking at the bill
- API resale with a thin markup (if your costs are more than 40% of the price)
- Small clients (they prefer a flat fee)

**Who does this already:**

- Vapi
- ElevenLabs (text-to-speech, billed per character)
- OpenAI / Anthropic (billed per token)
- Twilio (billed per text message or per minute)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Voice agent: $0.05-0.50/min
- AI emails: $0.01-0.10 per email
- AI document processing: $0.50-5 per document
- AI chat: $0.001-0.05 per message

**Cash flow:** 🟡 variable: it grows with how much the client uses.

**Difficulty:** 🟡 medium (you need billing and usage tracking)

---

### Model 4: Consulting on retainer

**What it is:** the client pays **a fixed amount** every month for access to you or your team. The scope is defined, but it isn't a one-off project.

🎨 **Picture this:** a concierge doctor on a monthly membership. You call when you need to. You pay even when you don't call. What you're buying is peace of mind.

**When it works:**

- You're an expert in a narrow niche (AI rollout, writing prompts, AI automation)
- Long sales cycles (3-9 months to earn trust)
- Well-funded business clients
- A small number of large clients can be enough for a stable model

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- "Everything" consulting ("I do anything with AI")
- Clients with small budgets (under $2K a month)
- No proven results or case studies

**Who does this already:**

- AI studios and agencies
- McKinsey's AI practice
- Solo consultants

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Solo expert: $2-8K a month per client
- Small agency: $5-25K a month per client
- Large corporate client: $50-500K a month

**Cash flow:** 🟢 predictable, but hard to get started.

**Difficulty:** 🟡 medium (you need proven expertise and connections)

---

### Model 5: Free plan + paid plan (freemium)

**What it is:** the basic version is free. Advanced features and unlimited use cost money.

🎨 **Picture this:** Dropbox. A little storage is free, you upload files, you hit the limit, and you pay a monthly fee for much more space. Most people stay on the free plan, a small share pays, and that's what keeps the business going.

**When it works:**

- Network effect (the more users, the more useful the product)
- The product spreads on its own (users recommend it to each other)
- Serving free users costs almost nothing
- It's obvious what the paid version adds

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- AI requests cost money, so the free plan gets expensive
- Only 1-2% of users pay, so you need a huge stream of new people coming in
- There are no truly valuable paid features

**Who does this already:**

- Notion (a free plan with limits)
- Loom (a free plan with limits on video length)
- ChatGPT
- Cursor

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Free plan: capped at 5-20% of the full volume
- Pro plan: $9-39/month
- Team plan: $29-99/month per user
- Enterprise: $99-499/month per user

**Cash flow:** 🟡 slow ramp-up, then recurring payments.

**Difficulty:** 🔴 hard for AI products (the free plan gets expensive)

---

### Model 6: Marketplace and commission

**What it is:** you're the middleman between buyer and seller. You take a percentage of every deal.

🎨 **Picture this:** Airbnb doesn't build hotels. It connects hosts with guests and takes a fee on every booking.

**When it works:**

- A fragmented market (lots of buyers and sellers)
- The two sides don't trust each other (they need a middleman)
- Deals happen often
- 6+ months of cash in reserve (the chicken-and-egg problem: no sellers without buyers, no buyers without sellers)

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- A small market (fewer than 10K buyers and sellers)
- The middleman is easy to bypass (after the first deal, the two sides go direct)
- No trust and quality-control system of your own

**Who does this already:**

- Upwork (a service fee on each job)
- Fiverr (a service fee on each order)
- Etsy (listing fees plus a share of each sale)
- PromptBase, an AI prompt marketplace (a commission on each sale)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Commission: 5-30% of the deal
- Listing fee: $5-50
- Seller subscription: $29-99/month

**Cash flow:** 🟡 variable until you reach critical mass.

**Difficulty:** 🔴 very hard (the chicken-and-egg problem is where most of them break)

---

### Model 7: Membership and community (paying for access)

**What it is:** you pay to be **inside a community**: resources, useful connections, support from other members.

🎨 **Picture this:** a private members' club in Manhattan. You pay the initiation fee and you meet the right people. What you get isn't a "product," it's **access**.

**When it works:**

- A narrow audience with a deep interest in the topic
- The founder is the "face" of the community
- New resources and events come out regularly
- The connections are worth more than the content

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- The founder has no personal brand
- No regular live events or conversation
- The audience isn't very active

**Who does this already:**

- Indie Hackers (free)
- Trends.co
- Newcomer
- IndiePen.com
- Levels.fyi (paid plan)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Paid tier on top of a free one: $10-49/month
- Premium membership: $49-199/month
- Mastermind (a small private group with a facilitator): a rough guide of $500-5K a month per member
- One-on-one coaching + community: a rough guide of $2-15K a month per member

**Cash flow:** 🟢 recurring payments, slow ramp-up.

**Difficulty:** 🟡 medium (you need a personal brand and the skill to run a community)

---

### Model 8: Done-for-you service

**What it is:** you **do the work** for the client, and they just get the result. You don't teach, you don't advise: you do it and deliver.

🎨 **Picture this:** a cleaning service doesn't teach you how to clean. It shows up and cleans. The client pays for the result, not for the tools.

**When it works:**

- A repeatable deliverable (newsletters, posts, design)
- AI does the work in batches, and you control the quality
- A clear monthly scope
- Business clients with budgets

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- Everything has to be redone from scratch for each client (no way to scale)
- Clients want to learn to do it themselves, not get it done for them
- A margin under 40% (serving clients costs too much)

**Who does this already:**

- DesignJoy
- Newsletter Operator
- ContentBot.ai (subscription)
- AI-powered agencies that turn out several times more work than a traditional shop

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- One deliverable a month: $500-1,500
- Several deliverables a month: $1.5-5K/month
- Premium done-for-you service: a rough guide of $5-15K a month per client

**Cash flow:** 🟢 recurring payments, and you can manage the workload.

**Difficulty:** 🟢 easy (to start with one client) | 🟡 medium (to scale: you need well-oiled processes)

---

### Model 9: Build to sell (build a product, sell it to a buyer)

**What it is:** you build a product, get it to steady revenue, and sell it. The sale price depends on revenue, growth, client retention and the market.

🎨 **Picture this:** flipping houses. Buy, renovate, sell. You don't live in it and you don't rent it out. You sell it.

**When it works:**

- It's clear who might buy a business like this
- A product that scales (not a service)
- A cycle of 6-18 months from building to selling
- Several successful sales turn into a portfolio of projects

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- A service business (buyers won't pay a high multiple for it)
- No revenue (a valuation "based on vibes" = $0)
- The product is the founder

**Who does this already:**

- Acquire.com (formerly MicroAcquire, a marketplace for buying small businesses)
- Flippa
- Empire Flippers

**Typical price (2026):**

- The valuation depends on revenue, growth, client retention and market conditions
- Talk through typical multiples with a business broker or on business-for-sale marketplaces

**Cash flow:** ❌ negative until the sale, then ✅ a lump sum at closing.

**Difficulty:** 🔴 hard (you need lots of different skills) | 🟢 the payoff is one deal instead of recurring payments

---

### Model 10: Hybrid model (subscription + usage fees + projects)

**What it is:** a mix of models: a base subscription + usage charges + one-off projects now and then.

🎨 **Picture this:** Verizon. A base plan (subscription) + overage charges for data (pay as you go) + buying a new phone (a project). Several sources of money.

**When it works:**

- Clients have different needs
- A mature business with several offers
- You have something to upsell
- You need to raise the LTV of each client

**When it DOESN'T work:**

- Early stage (you need to focus on one thing)
- Complicated bills confuse clients, and confused clients churn
- No proper bookkeeping

**Who does this already:**

- HubSpot (free plan + paid tiers + per-seat pricing + custom enterprise terms)
- Stripe (subscriptions + per-transaction fees + enterprise terms)
- Notion (Free + Pro + Plus + a team plan priced per seat)

**Typical price to the client (2026):**

- Base plan: $29-99/month
- Usage charge: $0.05-2.00
- Project: $1-10K
- What one client pays over their whole lifetime (LTV): a rough guide of $5-50K

**Cash flow:** 🟢 several sources.

**Difficulty:** 🟡 medium (only for a mature business)

#### 1. AI email assistant for lawyers

- **What it does:** drafts emails to clients, summarizes long threads, sets follow-up reminders (all of it only after the lawyer approves)
- **Who the client is:** solo attorneys and small law firms (1-10 lawyers)
- **How you get paid:** a monthly SaaS subscription (software you pay for monthly), $79-249 a month per seat
- **Tools:** Claude API (a way to plug Claude into your own program) + a Gmail or Outlook add-on + Calendly + Stripe
- **Difficulty:** 🟡 medium (you need to understand how a lawyer's work is organized)
- **Competition:** 🟢 low (big firms have their own tools, while small firms are offered almost nothing)
- **Why it works in 2026:** lawyers spend 40% of their time on email; AI saves 6 hours a week, and an hour of a lawyer's time costs the client $300
- **Related lessons:** [Choosing a niche](38b-niche-selection-methodology.md), [Pricing](39e-pricing-strategy-deep.md), [Choosing your path](49b-choose-your-path.md)

#### 2. AI listing description generator for real estate agents

- **What it does:** you upload photos and get a listing description, SEO copy (to help it show up in search) and social media posts, ready to publish
- **Who the client is:** real estate agents (independent agents or brokerages with 5-50 agents)

#### 4. AI visit notes for doctors (with the doctor in charge)

- **What it does:** records the conversation during a visit, turns it into structured SOAP notes, and sends them to the electronic health record (after the doctor approves)
- **Who the client is:** small clinics and doctors in private practice (the US and UK markets, both strictly regulated)
- **How you get paid:** $299-999 a month per doctor
- **Tools:** Whisper API + Claude + integration with electronic health record systems (Epic or Oracle Health, formerly Cerner, through an integration service such as Redox) + HIPAA-compliant storage (HIPAA is the US law that protects patient health information)
- **Difficulty:** 🔴 hard (HIPAA, a business associate agreement (BAA) to protect health data, and a mandatory legal review)
- **Competition:** 🟡 medium (Abridge and Nuance work with large hospitals, but there's little on offer for small clinics)
- **Why it works in 2026:** doctors spend 2 hours a day on paperwork; AI saves 1.5 hours, which is about $400 a day per doctor

## Section D. 4 types of AI business

🎨 **Picture this:** there are 4 types of business owners. First figure out **which one you are**, and only then pick a model.

---

### Type 1: Solo operator

**Profile:**

- 1 person (you)
- How many clients you can handle: 5-10
- Investment: $50-200 a month on tools

**How the work feels:**

- Complete freedom (no team to manage)
- High margins (80-90%)
- Limited growth: you're still selling your time
- 20-40 hours of work a week

**Ideas that fit:**

- #4 Consulting on retainer
- #8 Done-for-you service
- One-person agencies (ideas #16, #18, #24, #82)


**When to choose it:**

- Freedom matters more to you than wealth
- You like working on your own
- You want to start with no team and no capital
- What you want most is a business that fits around your life

**When NOT to choose it:**

- You want to sell the business someday
- You want passive income
- You want to grow the business beyond one person

**Examples:**

- DesignJoy (a design subscription)
- Marc Lou (several small SaaS products)

---

### Type 2: Small agency

**Profile:**

- 1-5 people + AI agents
- Investment: $200-1,500 a month on tools + $10-50K a year on hiring

**How the work feels:**

- Less hands-on work (you manage the team)
- Lower margins (40-60%, because of team costs)
- Processes can be scaled
- 30-50 hours a week at the start, 20-30 once you learn to delegate

**Ideas that fit:**

- All the ideas in Category 2 (content and marketing)
- All the ideas in Category 3 (customer support and sales)
- AI agency for local businesses (Stack 6)
- AI online course studio (Stack 7)


**When to choose it:**

- You want to grow beyond what one person can do
- You like managing people and building processes
- You want a team of your own
- You're ready to hire remote assistants and contractors

**When NOT to choose it:**

- You can't stand managing people
- You want to build a business fast and sell it
- You want passive income

**Example (an illustration):**

- Small agencies of 3-5 people: a team plus AI agents

---

### Type 3: SaaS founder

**Profile:**

- A team of 1-3 people
- Investment: $50-500 a month on tools + investment in the product

**How the work feels:**

- A heavy workload in the first 1-2 years (you're building the product)
- A lighter load from year three (you're scaling)
- Growing the value of the business matters more than a comfortable lifestyle
- 40-60 hours a week at the start

**Ideas that fit:**

- All the vertical SaaS products (Category 10)
- AI practice management (idea #101)
- AI new-patient intake (idea #102)
- "Build to sell" products (Category 9, for first attempts at SaaS)


**When to choose it:**

- You want a truly scalable business
- You love building products
- You're ready for a long ramp-up
- You're open to raising investment

**When NOT to choose it:**

- You need money right now
- You don't like technical work
- You don't like sales, cold emails and calls
- You want a business that fits around your life

**Examples:**

- Linear (a work tool for teams)
- Notion (raised investment, valued in the billions of dollars)
- Cursor (an AI code editor)

---

### Type 4: Build to sell

**Profile:**

- A team of 1-2 people
- Investment: $100-500 a month
- Timeline: 6-18 months to build, then sell

**How the work feels:**

- Work in short, intense sprints
- Several projects in progress at once
- A portfolio approach
- 30-50 hours a week

**Ideas that fit:**

- All the "build to sell" ideas (Category 9)
- Some AI tools for e-commerce (Category 7)
- Narrow tools for a specific niche


**When to choose it:**

- You enjoy building more than running something that's finished
- You have a line of ideas waiting
- You're fine with occasional large payouts instead of a steady stream
- You're ready for the path of a serial entrepreneur

**When NOT to choose it:**

- You need a regular, stable income
- You don't like negotiating deals
- You don't want a buyer going through your business (due diligence) before a sale
- You want to bet everything on one big project

**Examples:**

- Pieter Levels (Nomad List, Remote OK and other projects)
- Tony Dinh (sold several small projects)

## Section E. 30 niches with few offers so far (2026)

🎨 **Picture this:** in big cities, there's a restaurant on every corner. In the suburbs and small towns, the market is wide open. AI businesses work the same way. **The crowded niches are already taken. Where there are few offers, the market is open.**

### United States, set 1 (5 niches)

#### Niche 1: privacy-first AI for notaries and small law offices

**Why there's an opportunity:**

- Notaries, loan signing agents and small law offices handle IDs, contracts and other confidential documents every day
- Many of them are wary of pasting client documents into consumer AI tools
- The big legal AI tools are built and priced for large firms
- A price of $100-500 a month to the client is a reasonable illustration

**Tools:**

- An AI plan whose data-handling terms fit client confidentiality (read them before you sell anything)
- Connections to e-signature and practice tools (DocuSign, Clio)
- Privacy built in from day one: no Social Security or ID numbers in prompts, an access log, a clear written data policy

**Ideas that fit:** #1, #10

---

#### Niche 2: AI for 1099 freelancers and gig workers

**Why there's an opportunity:**

- More and more people work for themselves as freelancers, independent contractors and gig workers
- Taxes are the headache: receipts, deductions, mileage and estimated payments all need to be organized before tax time
- Existing apps mostly cover the basics (tracking expenses)
- A price of $5-30 a month to the client is realistic

**Tools:**

- Bank and card connections (Plaid)
- Receipt capture and categorization
- Clean exports for the person's accountant or tax software (the tool organizes the numbers; tax advice comes from a tax professional)

**Ideas that fit:** #41, #42

---

## Section F. A 12-month plan (example for a solo founder)

🎨 **Picture this:** a marathon, not a sprint. You run the first few miles slowly, and over the last few you're passing everyone. Consistency matters more than intensity.

**The scenario:** you work alone, running an AI services agency for local businesses (idea #18, social media management for local companies). This is an example of the process, not a forecast of results: how many clients you get and how much revenue you make depends on the niche, the city, the price and your work. There are deliberately no income numbers here.

---

### Month 1: The niche and conversations with clients

**Goal:** choose a specific niche and hold 5 conversations with potential clients

**What to do:**

- Week 1: read this lesson, pick 3 ideas and narrow them down to one (AI social media management for local restaurants)
- Week 2: find 50 potential clients in your city (lists from Google Maps and Yelp)
- Week 3: cold messages and calls; set up 10 conversations
- Week 4: hold 5 conversations and write down the problems the clients name

**Result for the month:**

- A detailed one-page ICP (ideal customer profile)
- A confirmed client problem (or a change of direction)
- A first sense of price: how much local restaurants are willing to pay for a service like this (ask on your calls)

**Costs:** subscriptions to tools (an AI assistant, a calendar). Current prices: [What's current](https://aimayak.com/now/)

**Common mistake:** going broad ("AI marketing for small businesses"). Stick to a narrow niche.

---

### Month 2: A minimal service and the first sale

**Goal:** put together a minimal version of the service and sign one paying client

**What to do:**

- Week 1: build a service template: 20 social media posts a month, Google Business Profile management and a monthly report
- Week 2: make a simple website (Carrd) and a meeting booking page (Calendly)
- Week 3: work your potential clients and turn one of the conversations into a payment
- Week 4: deliver the first month of work for the client

**Result for the month:**

- 1 paying client
- A step-by-step playbook for delivering the service
- A first case study (early results)

**Costs:** add tools for scheduling posts and for design

**Common mistake:** trying to sign 5 clients at once. Focus on one: quality beats quantity.

---

### Month 3: Refinement and new clients

**Goal:** do the work, improve the service and take on a couple more clients

**What to do:**

- Week 1: deliver the second month for your first client (with improvements based on their feedback)
- Weeks 2-4: find clients and hold 2 more conversations that end in a signed agreement
- Keep working with your first client through the third month

**Result for the month:**

- A refined offer
- Better processes (templates, checklists)

**Common mistake:** charging too little because "I'm still proving I'm worth something." Clients pay for value, not for your confidence.

---

### Month 4: Processes and repetition

**Goal:** document your processes, automate part of the work, take on new clients

**What to do:**

- Week 1: write down every step of the work in Notion
- Week 2: find what you can automate (post scheduling in Buffer, templates in Canva)
- Weeks 3-4: take on new clients (referrals are already helping)

**Result for the month:**

- A documented work process
- The share of the work that templates and automation now handle (measure it yourself)
- Your first referrals

---

### Month 5: Revisiting prices and raising quality

**Goal:** revisit your prices for new clients and keep your current ones

**What to do:**

- Week 1: set new prices for new clients (raise them by a step you can justify with results)
- Weeks 2-4: take on new clients at the new price; keep current clients on their existing terms

**Result for the month:**

- Price list, version 2
- Better unit economics (see the [Unit economics](99b-unit-economics-deep.md) lesson)

---

### Month 6: A stability check

**Goal:** check whether the process holds up consistently

**What to do:**

- Take on 1-2 more clients at your current price
- Publish your first case studies

**Result for the month:**

- 6 months of steady work
- Honest numbers: how many hours each client takes, what your margin is, how much of a safety cushion you have

**Checkpoint:** decide how much more time to invest based on your own numbers (workload, margin and safety cushion), not on other people's stories.

---

### Months 7-9: Careful growth

**Goal:** take admin work off your plate without losing quality

**What to do:**

- Consider a virtual assistant for admin tasks (compare the cost with the hours it frees up)
- Raise prices again only if demand and results support it
- Take on new clients as long as quality holds

**Result:**

- Fewer hours per client
- Margin tracking for each client

---

### Months 10-12: Stability and protection from competitors

**Goal:** steady work and first steps toward an advantage competitors find hard to copy

**What to do:**

- Consider a virtual assistant who prepares content
- Launch a referral program (with a reward you can afford)
- Narrow the niche even further (only Italian restaurants? or one specific city?)
- Start content marketing (a YouTube channel about AI for restaurants)

**Result:**

- Your personal brand starts to take shape
- Clients start coming to you on their own

---

### The 12 months in review

| Month | What you did | Process result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Client conversations and research | ICP, a confirmed problem |
| 2 | First client | A service playbook |
| 3 | New clients | Templates, checklists |
| 4 | Building processes | A documented work process |

| 6 | Stability check | Honest numbers |
| 7-9 | Careful growth | Fewer hours per client |

**This is an example of the process.** The outcome depends on the niche, the market and your work.

## Section G. 5 reasons AI businesses shut down

🎨 **Picture this:** many restaurants close within their first few years. AI businesses shut down for similar reasons, only everything happens faster.

---

### Reason 1: The wrong niche (too broad, no urgency, no budget)

**Symptoms:**

- "AI for all small businesses" (too broad)
- "Useful, but not critical" (no urgency)
- "Sounds great, but not right now" (no budget)

**Example (an illustration):**

- "AI process automation for small businesses" gets 0 signups
- By comparison: "AI receipt processing for criminal defense attorneys" gets 50 signups in 30 days

**How to fix it:**

- Narrow your niche on three levels: industry, then role, then a specific problem
- Talk to 20 potential clients BEFORE you build
- Check urgency (on a scale of 1 to 10, 8 and above means urgent)
- Check budget (if they already pay for some solution, the budget is there)

**More on this:** the lesson on choosing a niche

---

### Reason 2: Building alone, without feedback from real clients

**Symptoms:**

- 6 months of development, 0 clients
- "I'll launch when it's perfect"
- No conversations with potential clients at all

**Example (an illustration):**

- 6 months polishing an AI tool to perfection leads to zero interest
- By comparison: the same tool built in 1 week leads to 5 paying clients and improvements based on their feedback

**How to fix it:**

- A "concierge MVP" (MVP means minimum viable product, the smallest version that works): do the work for the client by hand first
- Build it, test it with one client, improve it
- Sell BEFORE you build (if 5 clients agree to pay, then build)
- Talk to clients every week

---

### Reason 3: A price that's too low (the $9/month subscription trap)

**Symptoms:**

- A price of $9-29 a month to the client
- You need thousands of clients just to break even
- High churn (people barely use the product)
- Serving a client costs more than they pay

**Example (an illustration):**

- An AI tool at $9 a month: even 200 clients don't cover the cost of serving and acquiring them
- The same tool at $99 a month: you need several times fewer clients to cover your costs, and you have enough time for each one

**How to fix it:**

- In B2B (selling to companies), charge from $99 a month
- Done-for-you services: from $500 a month
- Aim for the premium segment and quality, not volume
- Add value in layers (consulting together with the tool)

**More on this:** the lesson on pricing

---

### Reason 4: No attention to retention (only acquisition)

**Symptoms:**

- A constant chase for new clients
- Churn of 5-10% a month
- Clients stay less than 6 months, so LTV (how much a client pays you over their whole lifetime) is low
- Money burns on advertising

**Example (an illustration):**

- At 10% monthly churn, a client stays 10 months on average, so CAC (what it costs to win one client) has to be very low
- At 2% monthly churn, a client stays 50 months on average, so acquisition can cost more

**How to fix it:**

- Retention is what pays for acquisition
- Short weekly check-in calls with clients
- A well-designed start with each client (the first 30 days matter a lot)
- Surveys and upselling to current clients
- A discount for paying a year in advance (the client stays longer)

---

### Reason 5: Trying to grow before the product has found its market

**Symptoms:**

- Hiring a team while revenue is still small and unstable
- Spending on ads before clients start coming without them
- Adding features without validating the core one
- Building infrastructure before it's needed

**Example (an illustration):**

- Small revenue + hiring three people means cash drains away and the business shuts down
- The same revenue, handled differently: tidy up the processes, raise prices, wait for steady growth, and only then hire

**How to fix it:**

- Growing means turning what already works into a system
- Don't grow until your unit economics work; fix them first
- Hire a virtual assistant only when admin work takes more than 20 hours a week
- Scale up advertising only when a paid client pays back their acquisition cost in less than 6 months

**More on this:** the lesson on unit economics

---

### 📊 The 5 reasons compared

| # | Reason | Symptom | Time until shutdown | How hard to fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wrong niche | No interest | 3-6 months | Easy (change the niche) |
| 2 | No client feedback | Slow fade | 6-12 months | Medium (talk to clients more) |
| 3 | Price too low | Growth without profit | 6-18 months | Medium (raise the price) |
| 4 | No retention | An endless chase for clients | 12-24 months | Hard (build a process) |
| 5 | Growing too early | Cash drains away | 6-12 months | Hard (slow down) |

**Bottom line:** most failures can be prevented if you look honestly at your numbers and results.

## Section I. Where to find money to launch

> This is a general educational overview of funding options. It isn't financial or investment advice: for decisions about money, ownership stakes and contracts, talk to a lawyer and a financial advisor.

🎨 **Picture this:** starting a business is like choosing how to travel. A bicycle (your own money) is slow, but you do the steering. A semi truck is VC money (from a venture capital fund): fast, but the investors choose the direction.

---

### Path 1: Your own money (the best fit for most small AI businesses)

**How much you need:** $0-5K of your own

**Pros:**

- You own 100% of the business
- You move at your own pace
- No investors putting on pressure
- You choose how you work

**Cons:**

- Slow growth
- Limited resources
- Your personal money is at risk
- Nobody from outside holds you accountable for results

**When to choose it:**

- You work alone or with a small team
- A service business
- The done-for-you model
- The goal is a comfortable way of life, not fast growth

---

### Path 2: Friends and family

**How much you can raise:** $5K-50K

**Pros:**

- Lower stakes than with a venture fund
- The money comes from people who know you
- Flexible terms
- Decisions come faster

**Cons:**

- Personal relationships are on the line
- Not much money
- The paperwork is often sloppy (you need a lawyer's help)
- The pressure of responsibility: you don't want to let your loved ones down

**When to choose it:**

- You need $20-50K
- You need to hold out until your first paying clients
- You have a specific goal (hire a virtual assistant, test advertising)

**How to do it right:**

- Written deal terms (even simple ones)
- An ownership stake OR a convertible note (a loan that later turns into ownership)
- A 12-month term
- Keep them regularly updated on how things are going

---

### Path 3: Angel investors

**How much you can raise:** $50K-500K

**Pros:**

- "Smart money": advice comes along with the capital
- Access to connections
- Outside confirmation that someone believes in the idea
- Mentorship

**Cons:**

- They're hard to find for a small AI business
- It takes a lot of time (fundraising goes on for months)
- Your ownership stake shrinks
- You have to report to them constantly

**When to choose it:**

- SaaS (subscription software) that can scale
- A clear path to large recurring revenue
- You need $100K+ to build the product

**How:**

- A pitch deck for investors (10 slides max)
- Warm introductions through mutual contacts (cold emails rarely work here)
- Angel groups (look up the current networks and groups in your area)

---

### Path 4: Seed money from a venture capital fund

**How much you can raise:** $500K-3M

**Pros:**

- Big money
- High-level connections
- Help with scaling
- The fund's name adds credibility to the company

**Cons:**

- You give up a large stake (20-30%)
- They demand very fast growth
- The board of directors controls decisions
- A long process
- Only truly scalable SaaS fits

**When to choose it:**

- Real SaaS (not services)
- You can show the total addressable market (TAM) is $1B or more
- You already have proven demand and recurring revenue
- You're ready to build the company for 10 years

**How:**

- You need warm introductions through mutual contacts (cold emails to funds very rarely get a response)
- A strong founding team
- Early proof of demand
- A moat against competitors (something that's hard to copy)

---

### Path 5: Revenue-based financing

**How much you can raise:** $50K-1M

**Pros:**

- You don't give up any ownership
- You pay the money back from a share of revenue
- A fast process
- Fewer restrictions

**Cons:**

- More expensive than venture money (you pay back 1.3-2 times what you got)
- You need recurring revenue
- Only for a specific purpose (money for growth)
- A relatively new option

**When to choose it:**

- Your recurring revenue is already stable
- You know where to invest the money for growth
- You don't want to give up ownership

**Who provides this kind of money:** the market changes fast, so look for current companies in recent reviews

---

### 📊 Funding options compared

| Path | How much you can raise | Ownership you give up | Difficulty | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Your own money | $0-5K | 0% | 🟢 easy | Small AI businesses |
| Friends and family | $5-50K | 5-15% | 🟡 medium | Holding out until the first clients |
| Angel investors | $50-500K | 10-25% | 🔴 hard | Startups that can scale |
| Seed money from a VC fund | $500K-3M | 20-30% | 🔴 very hard | Real SaaS only |
| Revenue-based financing | $50K-1M | 0% | 🟡 medium | Growth after the product has found its market |

**The big picture:** a small AI business can usually get by on its own money. Outside money makes sense once demand has been proven.

**A sensible default for most beginners:** start with your own money, keep control, keep your options open.

## Section J. Checklist: are you ready to launch?

🎨 **Picture this:** before takeoff, a pilot runs through a checklist. Not because they forgot something, but because **checking against a list** is what keeps everyone safe.

15 items to check:

---

- [ ] **#1: The niche is specific (three levels deep)**
  - Industry, then role, then a specific problem
  - Example: "Lawyers" (broad), then "Immigration lawyers" (better), then "Solo immigration lawyers with Spanish-speaking clients" (specific)

---

- [ ] **#2: Your ICP (ideal customer profile) fits in one sentence**
  - Can you describe your ideal client in one sentence?
  - Example: "A solo immigration lawyer with 50-200 active cases, more than 80% of clients speak Spanish, practicing in California, Texas, Florida or New York"

---

- [ ] **#3: 5 real people who need this**
  - Names, companies, contact details
  - Not "lawyers in general," but specific people
  - Have you talked to them? At least three of the five?

---

- [ ] **#4: You've found paid alternatives people already use**
  - Clients ALREADY pay to solve this problem (another tool, a freelancer, their own staff)
  - Money is already being spent on this task
  - If nobody pays for it now, that's a bad sign

---

- [ ] **#5: The MVP (minimum viable product) scope is set: one week to build, max**
  - Can you build the core value in a week?
  - If it "needs three months," the scope is too big
  - Cut features and ship sooner

---

- [ ] **#6: Your tools are chosen**
  - Claude or OpenAI? Which specific services? Where will it all run (hosting)?
  - The decision is made, not "we'll figure it out later"
  - The tools match the MVP scope

---

- [ ] **#7: Your pricing is thought through**
  - A specific dollar amount, not "we'll see"
  - You've compared it with similar products
  - You're ready to say the price with confidence

---

- [ ] **#8: A product page or an outreach plan is ready**
  - A website on Carrd or Webflow is already live, OR
  - A cold email template is ready
  - You don't need both on day one; one is enough

---

- [ ] **#9: Your first 20 potential clients are identified**
  - Names, companies, email addresses
  - Not "we'll find them later"
  - You can start writing to them on day one

---

- [ ] **#10: 3 months of cash in reserve**
  - Personal savings cover 3 months of living expenses
  - OR a side job covers your basic costs
  - No "survive at any cost" pressure pushing you into bad decisions

---

- [ ] **#11: You've chosen a business structure (LLC, sole proprietorship and so on)**
  - In the US: most often an LLC or a sole proprietorship (ask an accountant or attorney which fits you)
  - Outside the US: it depends on the country
  - You don't need a complicated structure; a basic registration is enough

---

- [ ] **#12: Banking and payments are set up**
  - A business bank account is open
  - Stripe or PayPal is connected to accept payments
  - Business money is kept separate from personal money (for taxes)

---

- [ ] **#13: Contract templates are ready**
  - A statement of work template (what you'll do, by when, with what result)
  - A services agreement template
  - A nondisclosure agreement (NDA), if needed
  - Use existing templates; don't reinvent them

---

- [ ] **#14: You've thought through how to support clients**
  - How will you answer questions?
  - Have you promised clients a response time?
  - Through what channel (email, Slack, a ticketing system)?

---

- [ ] **#15: A stop rule at 90 days (if it doesn't work out)**
  - A stopping point decided in advance
  - Example: "If I don't have a single paying client by day 90, I change direction or stop"
  - This protects you from the "I've put so much in, it's a shame to quit" trap

---

### 📊 Your readiness score

**Count your ✓ marks:**

- **13-15:** 🟢 Ready to launch: you can start
- **10-12:** 🟡 Almost ready: close 2-5 gaps and launch
- **7-9:** 🟠 A bit early: slow down and check demand again
- **<7:** 🔴 Too early: keep studying the market and talking to potential clients

**Under 12? Close the gaps first.**

---

## 🎯 Bottom line

🎨 **Picture this:** the AI market changes fast. In a few years, some of these ideas will be ordinary work, and some will be out of date. That's why the habit of testing demand quickly is worth more than any one idea.

**What to do:**

1. **Pick ONE idea** out of the 110 (not "this one and that one too")
2. **Talk to 20 potential clients** before you build anything
3. **Check that the problem is real and that there's a budget to solve it**
4. **Build an MVP in 1-2 weeks**
5. **Find your first paying client** as soon as you can after testing demand
6. **Improve it based on real feedback**
7. **Run the numbers** (price, costs, retention) before you scale
8. **Scale what works**

**What not to do:**

- Don't build a product in isolation from clients
- Don't ignore how much clients are willing to pay
- Don't scale before the economics are proven
- Don't stay on your own money forever if your SaaS is already growing steadily
- Don't go to a venture fund if you have a service business or a lifestyle business

**The main principle:**

> "In the AI era, the one who does well isn't the smartest or the hardest-working, but the one who ships work faster and talks to clients more often."
