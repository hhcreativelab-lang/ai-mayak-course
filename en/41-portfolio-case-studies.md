# Portfolio and case studies: how to show your value

**Time:** about 25 min reading + 40 min practice

The numbers and clients in the examples below are made up to show how a case study is put together. In your own case studies, write only real results, don't round in your favor, and publish them only with the client's permission.

---

## The gist

Nobody hires an architect who just says "I know how to build houses." Good architects show photos of houses they've built, with the address, the cost and what the owner had to say. Your portfolio is the photos of your AI automations. Without it, you're just one more person who "knows how to do AI."

---

## Key concepts

- **Case study**: a structured write-up of a project: Problem → Solution → Result
- **Before/After ROI** (return on investment): specific numbers before and after (hours, money, speed)
- **Golden number**: the one main number that answers "so what?" ($X a month saved, N hours freed up)
- **Screenshots vs. descriptions**: screenshots convince, descriptions explain; you need both
- **3 types of clients**: small businesses, agencies, enterprise; each one wants something different
- **Portfolio platforms**: Notion, GitHub (a README page that describes a project), a personal website

---

## Theory

### Why words don't work and numbers do

Look at two ways to describe the same project:

**Version A (words):**
"I built an email automation system for a real estate brokerage. The system intelligently handles incoming inquiries and responds to them."

**Version B (a case study):**
"A real estate brokerage was getting 80+ inquiries a day and spending 4 hours on manual replies. I built a system that automatically answers the standard questions (65% of inquiries), routes anything urgent to a manager and logs everything in the CRM. The result: a team of 3 saved 18 hours a week, and the time to first response dropped from 4 hours to 3 minutes."

The second version sells. The first one only describes.

🎨 **Picture this:** an architect's résumé with no photos of houses vs. a résumé with 10 photos of real projects, with addresses and construction costs. The first is words. The second is proof.

---

### Case study structure: Problem → Solution → Result

🎨 **Picture this:** a case study is built like a Hercule Poirot mystery. First the crime (the Problem): who, what, when, what was lost. Then the investigation (the Solution): what was built and how. Then the reveal (the Result): the culprit is caught, the client is happy, the numbers are on the table.

**1. Problem (2–3 sentences)**

Who the client is, what hurts, and how it shows up in numbers:

```
Client: a digital marketing agency, team of 8
Problem: every Monday, about 7 hours went into putting together weekly
reports for 12 clients. The data was collected by hand from 4 different
sources: Google Analytics, Meta Ads, Google Ads and the CRM.
```

**2. Solution (4–6 sentences)**

What was built, with which tools, and how it works:

```
Built an automated pipeline: every Sunday at 11 p.m. the system
connects to the APIs of all four platforms and pulls the week's metrics,
Claude writes the narrative part with an analysis of the trends, and
the system assembles a PDF and sends it to the clients. The account
manager gets a Slack notification with a preview and can make edits
or approve sending with one click.
```

**3. Result (specific numbers)**

```
Before: 7 hours a week ≈ 30 hours a month of manual work
After: 15 minutes a week to review and approve all 12 reports ≈ 1 hour a month
Saved: 29 hours/month = $1,450/month (at a manager's rate of $50/hour)
Project cost: $2,800
Payback: under 2 months
```

### The golden number: one number that decides it

🎨 **Picture this:** the golden number works like a price tag. Without one, a shopper picks up the item, turns it over, thinks it over and puts it back. With a price tag, it takes one glance and the decision is made. One number clears up most of the doubt.

A potential client is reading your case study. They give it 10 seconds. They need to see ONE number that answers "why would I need this?"

Examples of golden numbers:
- **$3,200/month saved**: for clients who think about money
- **22 hours freed up a week**: for clients who think about time
- **Response time: from 4 hours to 3 minutes**: for clients who think about quality of service
- **87% of inquiries handled without a person**: for clients who think about operational efficiency

The golden number belongs in the first paragraph of the case study and in the headline.

**Case study headline template:**
```
[Type of business] saves [Golden Number] with AI automation of [process]

Examples:
"Real estate brokerage saves 18 hours a week by automating incoming inquiries"
"Digital agency cuts reporting time from 30 hours to 1 hour a month"
"B2B company speeds up lead response from 4 hours to 8 minutes"
```

### Screenshots vs. descriptions

🎨 **Picture this:** a screenshot is the physical evidence in a courtroom. Words are the witness testimony. One backs up the other. Without a screenshot, you're just telling a story. With one, you're showing the evidence.

A description explains the logic. A screenshot proves it's real.

**What to screenshot:**
- A chatbot in action, on a website or in Slack (showing real messages, with personal details blurred)
- A Google Sheet with a log of rows that were filled in automatically
- A finished PDF report the system generated (with client names blurred)
- A dashboard with metrics (if you built one)
- The workflow as a diagram (Claude Code can draw one in Mermaid, a text-based diagram format)

**What to blur out:**
- Real client names → replace them with "Client A," "Client B"
- Email addresses, phone numbers, personal details
- The client's financial data (show only relative numbers)

**The rule of three screenshots:**
At least three screenshots per case study: before (the manual process or the empty system), during (the system at work), after (the result in the data or in the report).

### 3 types of clients and what matters to each

🎨 **Picture this:** three types of clients are like three different passengers getting into a cab. Small business: "get me there fast, and don't break down." Agency: "reliable, with a receipt, and able to handle more trips." Enterprise: "show me your insurance, your license and your SLA before I get in."

**Small business (1–10 people):**
- Values: the owner's time saved, simplicity, reliability
- Worries: too complicated, it'll break, I won't be able to figure it out
- Golden number: "X hours a week you can spend growing the business instead of on routine work"
- Case study format: short, conversational, with a specific scenario: "a day without the automation vs. a day with it"

**Agencies (10–50 people):**
- Values: scalability, integration with the tools they already use, no need to hire someone new
- Worries: depending on one developer, no documentation
- Golden number: "Capacity for X clients without hiring another manager"
- Case study format: technical details, integrations, SLA (a service-level agreement: the response times you commit to), handoff documentation

**Enterprise (50+ people):**
- Values: security, compliance, ROI the CFO can sign off on, scale
- Worries: security risk, vendor lock-in, no support
- Golden number: "Annual ROI of $X on an investment of $Y"
- Case study format: formal, with an ROI calculation, mentioning security and documentation

### Where to keep your portfolio

🎨 **Picture this:** a Notion portfolio is like a small pop-up shop. You open it, lay out your goods nicely, flip the sign to "Open," and customers walk in. A personal website is like leasing your own storefront. It makes sense once you have plenty to show and a steady stream of customers.

**Option 1: Notion (recommended to start)**

Simple, good-looking, and you can publish it as a link. Structure:

```
Notion page your-portfolio
├── About (2 paragraphs + LinkedIn)
├── Services (what you offer)
├── Case Studies
│   ├── Case 1: Real estate brokerage, 18 hours/week
│   ├── Case 2: Digital agency, reports in 15 minutes a week
│   └── Case 3: B2B company, leads answered in 8 minutes
└── Contact (Calendly link)
```

**Option 2: A GitHub README**

If your clients are technical: a GitHub repo with a well-formatted README. It shows your code, your architecture and your professionalism.

**Option 3: A personal website**

Makes sense once you have 3+ case studies and a steady flow of clients. Until then, Notion is faster and simpler.

### Case study template (ready to use)

```markdown
## [Headline with your Golden Number]

**Client:** [Type of business, size, no name]
**Industry:** [Real estate / Marketing / E-commerce / ...]
**Timeline:** [X weeks from kickoff to launch]

---

### Problem

[Client] was spending [X hours/period] on [specific process].
Pain point: [what exactly was wrong: slow, expensive, unreliable, tedious].
Cost of the problem: [X hours × $Y/hour = $Z/month lost].

### Solution

Built [type of automation] that [what it does, step by step].
Stack: [tools: Claude Code, Cloudflare Workers, Slack bot, ...].
Key decision: [one interesting technical detail].

### Result

| Metric | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Time spent on the task | X hours/week | Y minutes/week |
| Cost of the routine work | $X/month | $Y/month |
| Response time | X hours | Y minutes |

**Project cost:** $X
**Payback:** X months
**Client testimonial:** "[Quote: only a real one, used with the client's permission]"

[Screenshot 1: before] [Screenshot 2: the system at work] [Screenshot 3: the result]
```

## Practice

**Exercise: write your first case study**

1. Pick a task you've already handled with AI: for yourself, at work or for someone you know (for example, from the "AI for everyday work" module: emails, meetings, presentations). If you've already built automations from the library lessons, use one of those

2. Fill in the Problem → Solution → Result structure:
   ```
   Problem: what task does the system take care of?
   Hours before: ___ a week
   Hours after: ___ a week
   Saved: ___ hours × $__ = $__ a month
   ```

3. Come up with your golden number: one number for the headline

4. Take 3 screenshots: before (how it was done by hand), during (the system or AI at work), after (the result: a log, a report, a spreadsheet)

5. Write the case study using the template, no longer than one letter-size page

6. Create a Notion page and publish it: click Share at the top → open the Publish tab → click Publish

**Goal:** a finished, published case study with specific numbers and screenshots. It's the first brick in your portfolio.

## Common mistakes

- **No before/after numbers.** "I automated reports" is a description. "30 hours → 1 hour a month, $1,450/month saved" is a case study. Without before/after metrics, a case study doesn't sell.
- **Writing a "case study" with no screenshots.** Words describe, screenshots prove. At least 3 screenshots: BEFORE (the manual process), DURING (the system at work), AFTER (the result in the data).
- **Showing the technical side instead of the business result.** The client doesn't care that you used Cloudflare Workers. They care that they save $1,450 a month. Put the tech stack in small print at the bottom.
- **Not asking for a testimonial right after delivery.** The best moment to ask is when the client sees the working system for the first time. A month later, the excitement will have cooled off. (→ [Delivery and retention](46-delivery-retention.md): more on collecting testimonials)

## Key takeaways

> Words describe; numbers sell. Every case study needs specific numbers: hours, money, speed.

> The golden number is the one main number that answers "so what?" It belongs in the headline.

> At least three screenshots. Visual proof clears up doubts that text alone can't.

> A portfolio of three case studies matters more than a personal website. Case studies first, presentation later.
